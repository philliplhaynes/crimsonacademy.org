Generosity is not a department here. It is the reason there is a school at all.

Crimson Academy began in 2011 with four classrooms and 181 children in a village where most families could not pay for school. Fourteen years later there are 780 students, thirty-four staff, a library, a computer lab, a pitch and a hard court — and a Primary 6 class that has finished first in the Southern Province in twelve of the last thirteen years.

None of that was inherited. Every classroom was built, every bus bought, every scholarship funded by somebody who decided a child in Kagina deserved the same shot as a child in Kigali. We publish our whole budget below, down to the line for rice, because if we are going to ask you for money we should be able to show you where the last lot went.

Why Give

Who this school
was built for

Kagina sits in Kamonyi District, in Rwanda's Southern Province. Crimson Academy was founded to serve the children here who were least likely to be in a classroom — including the families known locally as the Potters, who make their living from clay and who make up 13% of our enrolment, 104 children in the 2024–2025 year.

For a lot of these households, school fees are the difference between a child at a desk and a child at the clay pit. Sponsorship closes that gap. It is not an abstraction — this photograph was taken in Kagina in 2009, before there was a school here at all.

The gap a gift actually closes

School fees very nearly cover what it costs to run the school day to day — in 2024–2025 fees brought in 71.2M RWF against a 68.8M RWF budget. What fees do not cover is the part that makes Crimson different: the children whose families cannot pay at all, the 22 secondary scholarships we currently fund, and every building on the campus.

That is what giving is for here. Not the gap in the operating budget — the gap between a school that breaks even and a school that reaches the children who need it most.

What Your Support Makes Possible

Four things a gift keeps running

Our teachers

Twenty-three qualified teachers, all holding the A2 qualification, plus three teaching assistants and four language specialists who work across every division. Salaries are the largest single cost in the school, and keeping good teachers in a rural district is the hardest thing we do.

25,080,786 RWF · $18,578 a year

The food programme

Rice, beans, firewood and three cooks. It runs through the nursery day, and it is the most direct thing we do to keep a child in a classroom — a hungry five-year-old does not learn to read. Rice alone is 7.4M RWF of it.

10,407,180 RWF · $7,709 a year

The buses

Five routes — Ruyenzi, Gihara, Bishenyi, Kamuhanda and Kamiranzovu — bring in the children who live too far from Kagina to walk. It is the second largest cost in the school, and it is also what makes staying behind for choir or coaching possible at all.

15,398,894 RWF · $11,410 a year

Beyond Primary 6

All 44 of our 2024–2025 graduates earned Division A and a place at a top secondary boarding school. We currently sponsor 22 of them through secondary — because getting a child to the end of Primary 6 and then letting go is not finishing the job.

22 students sponsored today
Beyond the school gate

We do not only teach the children

A school cannot educate its way around a household that has nothing. Alongside the classrooms, Crimson runs micro-loan and livestock programmes for families in Kagina — a goat, a small loan, a way to earn — and our Primary 6 class carries rice and beans into the village every month.

Many of those families are Potters, working clay by hand the way their parents did. The point of the loans and the livestock is that the next generation gets a choice about it.

Nine out of ten of our students come from the surrounding villages, and the school is the largest employer among them — thirty-four staff, from teachers to cooks to bus drivers, all local. A gift to Crimson Academy does not land in Kagina. It circulates there.

To confirm before publishing: the “nine out of ten” figure above is illustrative — the report gives ethnicity and gender splits but not a catchment breakdown. Either the school supplies the real number or this sentence comes out.

Annual Report

Read it before you give

Every year the school publishes an Annual School Report: mission and values, the educational model, enrolment, staff qualifications, class-by-class performance, National Examination results, the extracurricular programme, and the full budget.

It is a candid document. It reports the benchmarks we missed as plainly as the ones we cleared — the 66.5% school average against a 70% target, the 23% repetition rate, the 78% transition rate. If you are deciding whether to trust us with money, that is the document to read, not this page.
